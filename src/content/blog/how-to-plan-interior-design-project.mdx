A home interior project feels overwhelming mostly because it's unfamiliar — not because it's genuinely complicated. Broken into stages, it's very manageable. Here's how a well-run project actually flows, and where your decisions matter most.

## 1. Get your budget honest first

Before looking at a single design, decide a realistic range you're comfortable spending — and a small buffer (we suggest ~10%) for the inevitable "while we're at it" additions. A clear budget is the single most useful thing you can bring to a designer: it lets them design *to* your number instead of designing something you then have to value-engineer down.

If you're not sure what's realistic, our [cost guide for furnishing a 2BHK](/blog/cost-to-furnish-2bhk-chennai) gives an honest, itemised starting point.

## 2. Write a simple brief

You don't need design language. Just answer:

- **Who lives here**, and how do you actually use each room?
- **What's not working** in your current space?
- **What you love** — save a handful of reference images.
- **Non-negotiables** — a pooja space, a study corner, storage for a specific hobby.

This brief is what keeps the project anchored when choices multiply later.

## 3. First meeting and site measurement

A designer visits, measures the actual space, and discusses your brief and budget. Real measurements matter — designs drawn on assumed dimensions cause expensive surprises at execution. By the end of this stage you should have a shared understanding of scope.

## 4. Concept, layout and 3D

This is the creative core: space planning, the look-and-feel, and a 3D visualisation so you can *see* the room before anything is built. Spend time here — changes on screen are free; changes after manufacturing are not.

<Callout>
The golden rule of cost control: **make your big decisions early, while they're still just pixels.** Layout, storage volume and finish level are decided now — and they drive most of the final price.
</Callout>

## 5. Material selection and the detailed quote

You choose finishes, hardware and materials, and these feed into a detailed, itemised quote (a BOQ — bill of quantities). A good quote lists what you're getting line by line, so you can see exactly where money is going and adjust before committing.

## 6. Approvals, drawings and production

Once you sign off, the design is turned into precise manufacturing drawings — for factory production of modular units and for site work like electrical, false ceiling and painting. This stage is mostly invisible to you but is where quality is decided.

## 7. Execution on site

Manufactured units are delivered and installed; civil, electrical, ceiling, painting and finishing work happen in a coordinated sequence. Good site management — the right trade at the right time — is what keeps a project on schedule.

## 8. Snagging and handover

Before you move in, walk through with a "snag list": anything misaligned, a finish to touch up, a drawer to adjust. A reputable studio fixes snags before final handover, then walks you through care and after-sales support.

## How long does it take?

It depends on scope, but as a planning guide a full-home interior is typically measured in **weeks, not days** — design and approvals up front, then execution. Running some work in parallel shortens the calendar. We publish indicative timelines on our pricing page so you can plan around realistic dates, not optimistic ones.
